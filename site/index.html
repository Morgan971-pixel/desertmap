Context

What is a food desert?

The USDA definition

A census tract where residents have low income and low access to a grocery store: the nearest supermarket is more than 1 mile away in an urban area, or more than 10 miles in a rural one.

Food deserts are linked to higher rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. They aren't random; they cluster in communities that faced decades of disinvestment and discrimination.

We trained a model to predict which tracts qualify, then audited it for racial bias four independent ways.

The model

What we built

We trained a Random Forest classifier on the USDA Food Access Research Atlas 2019, which covers every census tract in the U.S.

The 9 features

Median Family Income Poverty Rate % Black Population % Hispanic Population % White Population % No Vehicle % SNAP Enrolled Population (2010) Urban / Rural

No redlining data, ZIP codes or neighborhood names. The model sees only modern socioeconomic and demographic statistics.

Random Forest

200 decision trees each vote on “food desert or not,” and the majority wins. It handles non-linear patterns and shrugs off outliers.

SHAP

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) scores how hard each feature pushed each prediction: how far did poverty rate move this tract toward “food desert”? That makes every prediction auditable, tract by tract.

80/20 train/test split

58,024 tracts trained the model. The other 14,507 stayed hidden until testing, so scores on them show how it does on data it has never seen.

The finding

The model remembered something it was never shown

AI trained on present data inherits past policy. An audit catches it. Deployment without one doesn't.

Between 1935 and 1940, the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) graded neighborhoods in more than 200 U.S. cities. Grade A (“Best”) went to mostly white, affluent areas. Grade D (“Hazardous”) went almost entirely to Black and immigrant neighborhoods, which then lost access to mortgages and investment for decades.

Those grades shaped where grocery stores opened, where roads went and which communities got public money. Our model trained on modern data and never saw a single HOLC grade. Then we laid its predictions over the 1930s maps.

Impact

What auditing changes

An F1 of … looks fine in aggregate. The audit shows what that number hides: the errors aren't random, and the communities that most need an accurate answer get the wrong one most often.

Without auditing

  • The …x false positive gap stays invisible
  • Nobody sees the HOLC pattern
  • Racial composition steers predictions unnoticed
  • Deployment turns historical discrimination into policy

With auditing

  • The …x false positive gap is found and documented
  • C and D tracts are …x more likely to be flagged than A and B tracts
  • SHAP explains every single prediction
  • Mitigations identified: equalized-odds calibration, adversarial debiasing
  • The model becomes evidence of the problem instead of fuel for it
Methodology
Data
USDA Food Access Research Atlas 2019: … census tracts, national scope.
Target
LILATracts_1And10, the federal food desert definition: low income and low access at 1 mile (urban) or 10 miles (rural).
Split
80% training (58,024 tracts), 20% holdout (14,507 tracts), stratified by target, random seed 42.
Model
Random Forest: 200 trees, max depth 20, class-balanced weights for the 5:1 imbalance.
SHAP
TreeExplainer on a representative 2,000-tract subsample of the test set, with check_additivity=False for speed.
HOLC crosswalk
Mapping Inequality v3 (Nelson et al., 2023), spatially joined to 2010 tract boundaries. Used only to group predictions afterwards, never as a training feature.
Fairness audit
(1) race-removed model delta, (2) false positive rates by quartile, (3) counterfactual racial composition probe, (4) HOLC overlay after prediction.
Limitations

The confounding objection

Redlined areas became low-income areas, and low-income areas have poor food access. So the HOLC overlap might reflect income, not race.

The counterfactual test checks exactly that. For every test-set tract we reset racial composition to the national median and held income, poverty rate and every other feature fixed. … of predictions changed. If the pattern were only income geography, removing race shouldn't move individual predictions.

Calling it “just income geography” doesn't escape the point either. You would still need to explain why that geography lines up with 90-year-old federal grading maps, and that explanation is the redlining story.

What we can and can't claim

This is observational work; it doesn't establish causation. What it shows: the pattern holds across four independent tests, racial composition carries signal beyond income and poverty, and that is reason enough to audit before any deployment that affects food access. Responsible deployment doesn't need the causal question settled. It needs the disparity disclosed.
